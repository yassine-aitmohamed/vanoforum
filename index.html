Science, philosophy and the mind

Research is also a way of thinking and a way of living. What justifies a scientific claim, what our theories tell us about the world, and how to keep a healthy mind through years of hard problems.

Science and philosophy

Philosophy

How science justifies its claims

Falsification is the scientist's favourite philosophy, but it is not how science works. Theories are tested together with assumptions, supported by evidence in degrees, and replaced in long programmes. Mathematics adds its own path, from examples and conjectures to proofs.

Read the guide →4 minPhilosophy

Are our best theories true?

Scientific theories predict with astonishing precision, yet history is full of successful theories that turned out false. Structural realism offers a way out. Theories may be wrong about what exists and still right about the mathematical structure of the world.

Read the guide →3 minPhilosophy

Science is a strong-link problem

In some problems the worst parts decide the outcome. In others only the best parts matter. Science is mostly of the second kind, and seeing it that way changes how to choose problems, how to judge others' work and how to spend a career.

Read the guide →3 minPhilosophy

Thinking on paper: research notebooks

Published papers hide how research was done. Notebooks keep it. From Newton's Waste Book to Faraday's numbered diaries, the great experimenters thought on paper. Here is what they did, and a simple notebook practice for your own research.

Read the guide →3 min

Mind and wellbeing

Wellbeing

Mental health during the PhD

Depression and anxiety are common among doctoral students, and much of the risk comes from how the work is organised. Know the evidence, recognise the warning signs early, protect what helps, and ask for help before things become serious.

Read the guide →3 minWellbeing

Impostor feelings in research

Many researchers feel they are frauds who will soon be found out, despite clear evidence of their competence. The feeling is common, it is not a diagnosis, and it can be managed. Separate feelings from evidence, keep a record of what you have done, and talk about it.

Read the guide →3 minWellbeing

Focus, rest and the unconscious work of research

Hard problems are solved in alternation. Concentrated work, then a step back while the mind keeps working, then new insight. Protect deep focus, accept that your time is finite, use breaks deliberately, and sleep on hard problems.

Read the guide →3 min

Reading papers

Reading

How to read a research paper

A three-pass method, a map of the results, and a one-page reading sheet. Read for what the paper proves and how, not line by line from page one.

Read the guide →4 minReading

Tools for finding, reading and managing papers

Where to search, how to follow new work, how to read PDFs efficiently and how to keep a library you can still use in five years.

Read the guide →4 minReading

Writing a literature review

How to search the literature systematically, organise what you read, and write a review that compares ideas instead of listing papers. Useful for a thesis chapter, a survey or the background section of a paper.

Read the guide →2 minReading

Skimming a paper in twenty minutes

You cannot read every paper in full. Read the few paragraphs that carry the story, then the figures and the topic sentences, and decide in twenty minutes whether a paper deserves a deep reading.

Read the guide →4 min

Writing papers

Writing

Writing your first paper in English

The anatomy of a research paper, the order in which to write it, and the sentence-level habits that make English academic prose clear, even when English is not your first language.

Read the guide →4 minWriting

Writing the abstract

An abstract is a short, exact advertisement for the paper. Start with what you prove, give every main contribution, use strong verbs, and leave the symbols out.

Read the guide →3 minWriting

Writing the introduction, the motivation and the contribution

An introduction tells a short story. Where the problem comes from, why it matters, what was missing, and what this paper adds, with the main results stated early and the proofs kept for later.

Read the guide →3 minWriting

Presenting results and proofs

How to organize sections, choose between Theorem, Proposition and Lemma, write proofs that read as prose, display equations, and keep the LaTeX source clean.

Read the guide →4 minWriting

Revising paragraphs, step by step

A method to revise a paragraph without losing your voice. Check the content first, then the structure, then the sentences, then the words. Keep your notation and your structure intact.

Read the guide →3 minWriting

Becoming a good scientific writer

Good writing is a habit, not a talent. Read for style, keep a phrasebook, write every day, get feedback, and revise in passes. A practical training plan for researchers who write in a second language.

Read the guide →3 minWriting

The academic phrasebook

Reusable English phrases for every part of a research paper, sorted by function. Announcing results, defining, comparing with earlier work, structuring proofs, hedging, and concluding.

Read the guide →3 minWriting

Writing the thesis

How to turn your papers and notes into a coherent thesis. Structure, the general introduction, chapters built from papers, writing schedule, working with the committee, and preparing the defence.

Read the guide →2 minWriting

What older papers teach about writing

Papers written a century ago were often easier to read than today's. Keep the precision of modern writing, and take back what older papers did well. Say why you did the work, give one main idea, and state honestly what you do not know.

Choosing a journal

Submission, cover letters and peer review

Posting a preprint on arXiv

Understanding peer review

What peer review is for, where it comes from, what it does well and what it does not. Knowing its limits helps you write papers that pass it, read reports calmly, and review fairly yourself.

Research method

Method

A research method, from the first idea to the preprint

Choosing a problem, working in examples, discussing the strategy before writing proofs, checking originality, keeping a research log, and writing to other researchers about their work.

Read the guide →4 minMethod

Giving a research talk

How to plan, build and deliver a research talk. Know the audience, follow the three-part structure, design clear slides or board work, rehearse, and handle questions.

Read the guide →2 minMethod

Collaboration and authorship

How to start and run a research collaboration, share work fairly, decide author order, and avoid the most common conflicts. Includes the conventions of mathematics and other fields.

Read the guide →2 minMethod

Managing your PhD

A year-by-year plan for the doctorate, how to work with your supervisor, keep a research journal, handle being stuck, protect your health, and finish on time.

Read the guide →2 minMethod

What a PhD expects from you

The unwritten rules of the doctorate. Own your project, bring questions rather than answers, ask whether a problem is worth solving, and make every expectation with your supervisor explicit.

Read the guide →4 minMethod

Getting better at research

Research is a set of skills that can be trained. Choose your own problems, train your taste by making predictions, read beyond the trending papers, write everything down, shorten the loop between idea and test, and find people who tell you the truth.

Building an academic career

Few doctoral graduates obtain a permanent academic position. Hiring committees decide on evidence, not potential. Know what evidence they look for, finish your projects, plan for the gaps in your record, and decide consciously whether to play.

Open-source tools

Small, private tools for researchers and teachers. They run on your own computer, need no account, and do one job well.

VanoAtlas

A local research workspace: projects, tasks, meetings, daily routine, notes, papers and LaTeX in one private app.

VanoLeaf

A LaTeX editor in the spirit of Overleaf that runs on your own computer: file tree, PDF preview, click-to-jump, offline, and no compile timeout.

macOS · Windows · Linux · MIT

Source on GitHubDownload

VanoLib Reader

A PDF reader for papers in the browser: click a citation to see the reference, preview figures in place, highlight, annotate and cite in five styles.
