Most new doctoral students arrive with the habits of a good student: solve the problem you are given, work harder when it does not work, and come back when you have the answer. These habits stop working in the first months of a PhD. Nobody tells you, because your supervisor has forgotten what the first year felt like and you do not yet know which questions to ask. This guide makes those unwritten expectations explicit.

## The PhD is your project

Financially, a doctorate rarely pays off. In the United Kingdom, for example, the salary premium over a master's degree is small, and the years on a stipend are years of lower income. What justifies the years is what you become: an independent researcher who understands a problem better than anyone else. Treat the PhD as a project you run, not as a job someone supervises.

- **You are not working for your supervisor.** They designed the project and found the money. You choose how to explore it, which angle to take, and what you want to learn from it.
- **Test whether you own it.** If you can answer every question about your project, technical or general, it is yours. If you still need your supervisor to answer some of them, it is not yours yet.
- **Present your own work.** Give talks on it, at seminars and conferences. People quickly see who did the work by the answers to their questions.

## What the supervisor does, and what you do

A supervisor provides the vision (where the project fits in the field, and why it matters), the resources, and the contacts with people who have the expertise you need. A supervisor also teaches, writes grants and runs several projects at once. Realistically, they can think about your project an hour or two each week. You can think about it every day.

So the motivation has to come from you. If your project leaves you cold, try three things before giving up on it:

1. **Dig deeper.** Find out why the question matters and to whom. Talk to people who care about it.
2. **Change the angle.** Look for a method, an example or an application that interests you, inside the scope of the project.
3. **Treat it as training.** Decide what skill you want to leave with: a technique, a computer tool, a way of writing.

If nothing works after months, stopping is a legitimate and mature decision, not a failure.

## Meetings are for questions

The most common first-year mistake is to avoid your supervisor until you have results. At the edge of knowledge, being stuck is normal. You do not need results to book a meeting. A precise question, a failed attempt or a counterexample is enough.

- Agree on what meetings are for, and how often they happen.
- Start each meeting with the big picture, then go into detail only as far as needed. Your supervisor knows the field, not the details of your computations.
- If you wrote three pages to prepare, cut them to half a page.

## Can do is not should do

In your studies, every exercise was worth solving because it counted for a grade. In research, you must decide what deserves your time. Before spending weeks on a proof, a computation or a program, ask: what will I learn, and does it move the project forward? A correct result can still be a waste of time.

Most of your ideas will be weak. That is normal. Expose them early to people who will criticise them, and let the weak ones go quickly. This is how you build research taste.

## Silence is not neglect

Supervisors are busy. An unanswered email usually means it was forgotten, not ignored. Send reminders, more often than feels polite. A cancelled meeting is rarely personal.

## Authorship and reviews

- **Discuss authorship early.** Do it before a collaboration starts, not when the paper is written. Conventions differ by field: in mathematics, authors are usually listed alphabetically. The guide on [collaboration and authorship](collaboration-and-authorship.html) explains them.
- **Reviews can be unfair.** A harsh report is a judgment of one text by one person on one day. Separate the quality of your work from the tone of the report, keep what is useful, and move on.

## Rest is part of the plan

There is always another deadline. If you do not schedule rest, you will not get it. After a submission or a missed deadline, take a day or two, then reflect on what went wrong and continue.

## Checklist

To work out for yourself:

- [ ] I know why I am working on my current problem.
- [ ] I have shown my latest idea to someone who could criticise it.
- [ ] I separate harsh feedback on my work from my worth as a researcher.
- [ ] I have scheduled rest in the coming weeks.

To discuss with your supervisor:

- [ ] We agree on what meetings are for and how often they happen.
- [ ] I know what enough progress looks like before the next meeting.
- [ ] We have discussed authorship for every ongoing collaboration.
- [ ] I know the best way to follow up when an email gets no answer.

## Summary

- The PhD is a project you own, not a job you are given.
- Your supervisor brings vision and resources. You bring the drive.
- Bring questions to meetings, not only results.
- Ask whether a problem is worth solving before solving it.
- Make every expectation explicit, early.
