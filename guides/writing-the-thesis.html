Writing the thesis

How to turn your papers and notes into a coherent thesis. Structure, the general introduction, chapters built from papers, writing schedule, working with the committee, and preparing the defence.

The thesis is the document that proves you can do independent research. It is also, for many readers, the most complete introduction to your topic. Plan it as a book, not as a collection of files.

Know the rules

Every university has rules: format, length, language, whether published papers can be included as chapters, deadlines for submission, composition of the committee. Read them at the start of your final year.

Two common formats

  • Monograph: a single unified text, organised by topic.
  • Thesis by publication: a general introduction followed by your papers as chapters, sometimes with linking sections.

Even in a thesis by publication, the general introduction is substantial work. It is the part that examiners and future readers read most closely.

The general introduction

The general introduction should:

  1. Present the field and the main questions for a non-specialist in your discipline.
  2. Explain the background needed to read the thesis.
  3. State your main results clearly, with their context.
  4. Explain how the chapters relate and in which order to read them.
  5. Mention open questions and future work.

Many theses also include a summary in the national language of the university.

Building chapters from papers

  • Unify notation across chapters, or explain the differences.
  • Remove repetitions of the same background, or move shared background to a preliminary chapter.
  • Add a short introduction to each chapter explaining its place in the thesis.
  • State clearly which chapters come from joint work and what your contribution was.

A writing schedule

  • Start early. Write the background chapter and parts of the introduction while you still do research.
  • Plan backwards from the submission date. Leave time for your supervisor to read each chapter and for revisions.
  • Write the introduction last, or rewrite it at the end, when you know exactly what the thesis contains.
  • Fix a weekly target (pages or sections) and track it.

A realistic plan for the final writing phase is several months, not weeks.

Technical tips

  • Use a standard thesis class or your university's template.
  • Keep one bibliography file for the whole thesis.
  • Use version control (Git) or regular backups in at least two places.
  • Compile the full thesis regularly to catch errors early.
  • Create an index of notation if your thesis uses many symbols.

The committee and the reports

Your supervisor usually proposes the reviewers. Send them a complete and polished version. Reviewers write reports, sometimes with corrections. Treat these like referee reports: correct what is wrong, clarify what is unclear, and keep a list of changes.

The defence

  • Prepare a talk adapted to the audience: committee, colleagues, sometimes family. Follow the three-part structure of Giving a research talk.
  • Rehearse with your group.
  • Prepare for questions on the whole thesis, the background, and future directions.
  • After the defence, make any required corrections and submit the final version.

Checklist

  • I know the rules of my university for format, length and deadlines.
  • My general introduction presents the field, the results and the plan.
  • Notation is consistent across chapters.
  • I have a writing schedule with time for my supervisor's comments.
  • My files are under version control with backups.
