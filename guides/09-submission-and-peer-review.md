Submitting a paper is the start of a conversation with an editor and one or more referees. It usually lasts months. A clean submission and calm, precise answers make it shorter.

## Before you submit

- **Read the paper one last time on paper**, from the title to the last reference.
- **Ask a colleague** who did not write it to read the abstract and the introduction. If they cannot say what the main result is, revise.
- **Post the preprint** on arXiv if your field does so and the journal allows it. It establishes the date of your result and makes it visible.
- **Prepare the files**: the PDF, the LaTeX source if required, figures, and the list of suggested referees if the journal asks for one.
- **Check the author information**: full names, affiliations, institutional email addresses, ORCID identifiers, and funding acknowledgments.

## The cover letter

Keep it short: three or four paragraphs, one page at most. The editor reads many of them.

1. **What you submit**: the title, the authors and the journal.
2. **What the paper does**: the main result in two or three sentences, in words, as in the abstract.
3. **Why this journal**: one sentence linking the paper to the journal's scope or to papers it published.
4. **Formalities**: the paper is not under consideration elsewhere, the preprint is on arXiv (with its number), and the suggested editor or referees if relevant.

> Dear Editor,
>
> We submit our paper "…" for consideration in *[Journal]*. In this paper, we prove that … We also show that … and, as an application, we …
>
> The paper continues a line of work published in *[Journal]*, notably [reference]. The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere. A preprint is available as arXiv:XXXX.XXXXX.
>
> Sincerely, …

## What happens next

1. **Editorial screening.** The editor checks the scope and the general level. A *desk rejection* at this stage is common and often fast. It is not a judgment on correctness.
2. **Referees.** The handling editor invites one or more referees. Finding them can itself take weeks.
3. **Reports.** Referees read the paper and write reports. This is the long part.
4. **Decision**: accept (rare at the first round), minor revision, major revision, or reject.

You can politely ask the editorial office about the status after a reasonable time, for example after six months without news in a slow field. One short, courteous email is enough.

## Answering the referees

A revision is accepted when the referees see that every point was taken seriously.

- **Thank the referees once**, briefly, at the start. Not in every answer.
- **Answer point by point.** Copy each comment, then write your answer under it. Say exactly what you changed and where: "We added Remark 3.4 on page 9", "The proof of Lemma 2.1 now treats the case $n = 0$ separately".
- **Change the paper, not only the letter.** If a referee misunderstood a passage, the passage probably needs rewriting. Other readers will misunderstand it too.
- **Disagree politely, with arguments.** You do not have to accept every suggestion. If you disagree, explain why, with a precise mathematical or scientific reason. Never be sarcastic.
- **Mark the changes.** Send a version with the changes highlighted, or a list of changes, if the journal allows it.

> **Comment 3.** The assumption that $X$ is compact seems unnecessary in Theorem B.
>
> **Answer.** We agree that it is not needed for the existence part. It is needed for uniqueness: Example 5.2 (new) gives two non-isomorphic solutions on a non-compact $X$. We have split Theorem B into two statements accordingly, see page 4.

## After a rejection

A rejection is part of the job, even for the best researchers. Wait a day before reading the reports again. Extract every useful comment, improve the paper, and submit to the next journal on your list.

## Writing a referee report yourself

Sooner or later, you will be asked to referee. A good report is precise and useful to both the editor and the authors.

1. **Summary**: what the paper claims, in your own words, in one paragraph. This shows the editor you understood it.
2. **Assessment**: is it correct, new, and significant for this journal? Give reasons.
3. **Major comments**: errors, gaps in proofs, missing references, overstated claims.
4. **Minor comments**: typos, unclear sentences, notation, listed by page and line.
5. **Recommendation**, in a separate confidential note to the editor if the journal allows it.

Be kind and specific. Criticize the paper, never the authors. If you cannot assess part of the paper, say so to the editor. Decline the invitation if you have a conflict of interest or no time to do it properly.

## Checklist

- [ ] Preprint posted, files and author information ready.
- [ ] Cover letter of one page with the main result in words.
- [ ] Answers to referees point by point, with the exact changes.
- [ ] Disagreements explained with arguments, never with irritation.
