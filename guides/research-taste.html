Getting better at research

Research is a set of skills that can be trained. Choose your own problems, train your taste by making predictions, read beyond the trending papers, write everything down, shorten the loop between idea and test, and find people who tell you the truth.

Nobody teaches research directly. You receive a problem, a desk and the instruction to find something new. Most people then copy what they see from the outside: papers, talks, announcements. They learn to look like researchers. The actual work is a stack of smaller skills, and almost every one of them can be trained on purpose. This guide complements the research method with the habits that make that method work better year after year.

Choose your own problems#

The physicist Richard Hamming liked to ask colleagues what the important problems of their field were, and then why they were not working on them. The question is uncomfortable because most of us do not choose our problems. We absorb them from a supervisor, a famous group or the paper everyone is discussing.

An absorbed problem comes without its reasoning. You know that others find it important, but not why, and not what would make them drop it. On a fashionable problem you also compete with many people who started earlier.

There are two ways to find a problem:

  1. Improve on the literature. Read papers and look for what can be generalised, sharpened or simplified.
  2. Start from what you want to exist. Choose a result or an understanding you genuinely want, and work backwards to the questions that lead there.

Both are useful. The second is the more reliable source of original work, because it takes you where no survey goes.

Train your taste#

Research taste is often described as a gift. It behaves more like a skill: it improves with predictions and corrections.

  • Predict before you check. Before computing an example or running an experiment, write down what you expect. Compare afterwards.
  • Guess the result from the method. Read the setup of a paper, hide the main theorem or the results section, and guess what it says.
  • Forecast importance. Note which new papers you think will matter in two years. Check your list later.

A few hundred predictions, each followed by a correction, change how you judge problems.

Improve what you read#

If you read only what everyone reads, you will have the same ideas as everyone, at the same time.

  • Read older work. Many fields rediscover their own past. A classic paper or a twenty-year-old survey often contains ideas that were never fully exploited.
  • Read outside your area. Methods travel between fields. A neighbouring subject is often where your next tool comes from.
  • Read the paper, not the summary. The details, the remarks and the section on limitations are where the real information is.

Make the problem smaller#

The mathematician and engineer Claude Shannon recommended a simple move when stuck: shrink the problem until it becomes almost trivial, solve the small version, then add the difficulty back one piece at a time. In mathematics, this means the smallest dimension, the simplest example, the most degenerate case. The guide on managing your PhD lists more strategies for being stuck.

Write everything down#

An idea feels complete until you try to write it. The page shows the assumption you never checked, the step that does not follow, the two claims that contradict each other.

Keep a research log. For every attempt, write the hypothesis, what you did, what you expected, what happened, and what you now believe. Darwin wrote down every fact that contradicted his theory immediately, because he noticed that he forgot those facts first. Your memory does the same with failed attempts.

Write some of it in public too: a clear expository note, a blog post, a talk. A clear explanation of a known result is a real contribution, and a body of clear writing shows how you think.

Shorten the loop#

Research speed is mostly the speed at which you find out that you are wrong. Make every test cheap:

  • Check an idea on the smallest example before attempting the general proof.
  • Keep your computations, code and notes organised, so that redoing a calculation takes minutes, not a day.
  • Try the rough version of every idea first. Let most of them fail early.

Look at the details#

A general argument can hide a mistake that one explicit example reveals at once. Compute examples by hand. Read the output of your programs, not only the final number. When something fails, collect the failures, sort them, and attack the largest group first.

Find your people#

Hamming also observed that colleagues who worked with their office door closed got more done each year, but those who kept it open did the more important work, because interruptions told them what mattered. Keep your door open: answer emails, go to seminars, talk about half-finished ideas.

Be generous. Share notes, code and corrections. Explain hard things simply. The returns come later and indirectly. A colleague who tells you an idea is bad before you spend three months on it is worth more than any tool.

Checklist#

  • I can say why my current problem is important, in my own words.
  • I wrote down a prediction before my last computation or experiment.
  • This month I read at least one older paper and one paper outside my area.
  • My research log records failed attempts, not only successes.
  • I tried the smallest case of my problem before the general one.
  • I showed an unfinished idea to someone who could criticise it.

Summary#

  • Choose problems for reasons you understand.
  • Taste is trained by predictions and corrections.
  • Read old work, other fields, and the papers themselves.
  • Shrink the problem, test cheaply, and write everything down.
  • Keep your door open and be generous.
