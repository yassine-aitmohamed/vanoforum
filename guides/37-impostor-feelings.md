You were admitted to a doctoral programme, your paper was accepted, you were invited to give a talk, and still you feel that everyone else understands more than you, and that sooner or later someone will notice. This experience has a name, and it is very common among researchers.

## What it is

The psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes described the **impostor phenomenon** in 1978, in high-achieving women who attributed their success to luck or to others' mistakes rather than to their own ability. It has since been found in men and women, at every career stage.

A systematic review published in the *Journal of General Internal Medicine* in 2020 found reported rates between 9% and 82%, depending on how it was measured. The review notes that it is not a recognised psychiatric diagnosis, but that it often comes with anxiety and depression and is linked to burnout and lower job satisfaction.

## Why research makes it worse

- **You work at the edge of what you know.** Feeling ignorant is the normal state of research, but it is easy to read it as personal inadequacy.
- **You compare your inside with others' outside.** You see your own confusion, failed attempts and drafts. You see others' polished talks and published papers.
- **Feedback is rare and mostly negative.** Referee reports list what is wrong. Rejections are frequent. Praise is rare.
- **Success feels unrepeatable.** Each new problem starts from zero, so past results seem to prove nothing about the next one.

## Feelings and evidence

The organisational psychologist Adam Grant offers a useful reframe. When you think others overestimate you, it is more likely that you underestimate yourself. Others have an outside view: they see your record and the progress you cannot see from the inside.

The practical step is to separate two questions:

1. **What do I feel?** That I do not belong, that I was lucky.
2. **What does the evidence say?** Admissions, results, papers, talks, the judgments of people who know the field.

Feelings are real, but they are not evidence. When they disagree, trust the evidence.

## What helps

- **Keep a record of your work.** A file of results, accepted papers, good feedback, problems you solved. Read it when the feeling comes.
- **Talk about it.** Ask senior colleagues whether they have felt it. Most have. Hearing it from someone you admire is the fastest cure.
- **Name the gap precisely.** "I am a fraud" cannot be fixed. "I do not yet understand spectral sequences" can be fixed with a book and two weeks.
- **Collect your best moments.** Grant recommends the Reflected Best Self exercise: ask a few people for stories of when you were at your best, and look for the common thread.
- **Treat confusion as normal.** If you understood everything, you would not be doing research.
- **Get help when it is heavy.** If the feeling comes with persistent low mood or anxiety, see [Mental health during the PhD](mental-health-phd.html).

## Checklist

- [ ] I keep a written record of my results and positive feedback.
- [ ] I have talked with at least one senior researcher about self-doubt.
- [ ] When I feel inadequate, I write down the precise gap and a plan to fill it.
- [ ] I separate what I feel from what the evidence shows.

## Summary

- Impostor feelings are common at every career stage, and they are not a diagnosis.
- Research makes them worse: constant confusion, rare praise, comparison with polished work.
- Separate feelings from evidence, and trust the evidence.
- Keep a record, talk about it, and turn vague doubt into precise gaps.
