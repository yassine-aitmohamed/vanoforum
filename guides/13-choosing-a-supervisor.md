For three to six years, your supervisor will shape your daily work, your first papers, your network and your first job. A brilliant researcher can be a poor supervisor, and a less famous one can be an excellent mentor. Choose the person, not only the topic or the university.

## What to look for

**Research fit.** You should find the supervisor's recent papers interesting enough to read them without being asked. Look at the last five years, not the most famous paper from twenty years ago.

**Active research.** Does the supervisor still publish, give talks, and have current students and postdocs? An active researcher brings you into an active network.

**Track record with students.** Look at former students: did they finish, did they publish, where did they go next? Many group pages list alumni. In mathematics, the Mathematics Genealogy Project lists most supervisors' former students.

**Supervision style.** Some supervisors give a precise problem and meet every week. Others expect you to find your own problem and meet once a month. Neither is wrong. What matters is that the style fits you, especially in the first year.

**Availability.** A supervisor with twenty students or major administrative duties may have little time. Ask how often students meet the supervisor.

**Funding and stability.** Is your funding secure for the whole thesis? Is the supervisor planning to move, retire or take long leave?

**Group and environment.** Are there other students, a seminar, a reading group? A good environment matters as much as the supervisor.

## Questions to ask the supervisor

- What kind of problem would I work on in the first year?
- How often do you meet your students, and how?
- Do your students write papers alone, with you, or both?
- How do you decide authorship on joint papers?
- What do your former students do now?
- What do you expect from a student in the first months?
- How is the funding organised, and what happens if the thesis takes longer?

Ask them calmly. A good supervisor is glad to answer.

## Talk to current students

This is the most useful step, and the one most applicants skip. Write to one or two current or former students (their emails are usually on the department page) and ask for a short call. Ask:

- How often do you meet, and are the meetings useful?
- Do you feel free to ask questions, including basic ones?
- How long do students usually take to finish?
- Would you choose the same supervisor again?

Students are usually honest in a private conversation. Listen to what they do not say as well.

## Warning signs

- Several former students left without finishing, with no clear explanation.
- Current students are vague or uncomfortable when you ask about supervision.
- The supervisor cannot explain what you would work on, or promises everything.
- Authorship conventions are unclear, or students' work is published without them.
- Funding is described as "probably available".
- You leave the conversation feeling small or ignored.

One warning sign is not a verdict. Several are.

## Co-supervision

Many theses have two supervisors: a main one and a second one, sometimes in another university or country (for example in a cotutelle). Co-supervision gives you two perspectives and a wider network. It works well when the roles are clear from the beginning: who you meet weekly, who reads drafts, who decides on the thesis topic.

## Your side of the relationship

A good supervision is a relationship in both directions. Come to meetings prepared, with one page of notes and precise questions. Send drafts early. Say when you are stuck. Keep a written record of what was decided. The guide [Managing your PhD](managing-your-phd.html) describes a weekly routine that makes meetings productive.

## Checklist

- [ ] I have read the supervisor's papers from the last five years.
- [ ] I know where former students went and whether they finished.
- [ ] I have asked about meetings, authorship, funding and expectations.
- [ ] I have talked to at least one current or former student.
- [ ] The supervision style fits the way I work.
