Writing a literature review

How to search the literature systematically, organise what you read, and write a review that compares ideas instead of listing papers. Useful for a thesis chapter, a survey or the background section of a paper.

2 min read · 5-item checklist · Not read yet

A literature review is not a list of summaries. It is an argument about the state of a field: what is known, how the results relate, and what is still open. Your reader should finish it with a map of the area.

Define the scope#

Write one or two sentences that say what the review covers and what it does not. A clear scope keeps the search manageable and the text focused.

Search systematically#

  • Start from two or three key papers and follow their references (backward search).
  • Use citation databases to find who cites them (forward search): Google Scholar, Semantic Scholar, zbMATH Open, MathSciNet if your library has access.
  • Search arXiv listings in your categories for recent preprints.
  • Read recent surveys and the introductions of recent theses.
  • Record your search terms and sources, so you can repeat or extend the search.

Organise as you read#

  • Use a reference manager (Zotero, JabRef, or a plain BibTeX file) from the first day.
  • For each paper, write a short note: main result, method, assumptions, relation to other papers.
  • Use tags for themes, methods and open questions.
  • Keep a table with one row per paper and columns for the dimensions you want to compare (setting, method, result, limitation).

Find the structure#

Look at your table and notes. Group papers by:

  • Theme: the different questions studied.
  • Method: the different approaches to the same question.
  • Chronology: when the history of an idea matters.

Usually a review combines these. Choose the structure that best explains the field to a newcomer.

Write to compare#

For each section, write about ideas, not papers. Compare:

  • "Both [A] and [B] obtain [result], but [A] assumes [condition], while [B] uses [method]."
  • "The approach of [A] was later simplified by [B]."
  • "These results leave open the case [case]."

Each paragraph should have a topic sentence that states a claim about the literature.

Identify the gaps#

End each section, and the review as a whole, with what is unknown. These gaps motivate your own work. Be fair: a gap is not a criticism of earlier authors.

Common mistakes#

  • A list of papers with one paragraph each.
  • Only recent papers, or only old ones.
  • Misreporting results you did not read carefully. Check every claim against the source.
  • Citing papers you did not read because other papers cite them.

Checklist#

  • My scope is written down in two sentences.
  • I searched backward, forward and in recent preprints.
  • I keep a comparison table and notes for each paper.
  • My review is organised by ideas, not by papers.
  • Each section ends with what is still open.
