Science is a strong-link problem

In some problems the worst parts decide the outcome. In others only the best parts matter. Science is mostly of the second kind, and seeing it that way changes how to choose problems, how to judge others' work and how to spend a career.

The psychologist Adam Mastroianni uses a simple distinction to explain why so much effort in science goes in the wrong direction. Some problems are weak-link problems: the result depends on the worst element. Others are strong-link problems: the result depends on the best element, and the worst barely matters.

Two kinds of problems#

  • Weak-link problems. Food safety: one contaminated product can kill. A car engine: the best spark plugs do not help if the gearbox is broken. You solve them by raising the floor, with rules, inspections and standards.
  • Strong-link problems. Music: you listen to the best albums and ignore the rest. Athletics at the Olympics: only the best athletes count. You solve them by raising the ceiling, by letting many attempts happen and finding the best.

Using the wrong method is costly. Inspecting every song before release would remove the bad music and most of the great music too.

Why science is a strong-link problem#

In the long run, science keeps its best ideas and forgets the rest. Phlogiston, the ether and the theory of tiny humans curled inside sperm cells are gone, while evolution, quantum mechanics and the calculus remain. A thousand forgettable papers do not cancel one paper that changes a field. Bad work can waste time and resources for a while, and fraud does real damage, but what matters most is how good the best work is.

Mastroianni argues that science is often run as if it were a weak-link problem: heavy screening of papers and grants designed above all to stop bad work. Such screening also tends to stop risky, novel and interdisciplinary work, which is where the strongest links come from. Several Nobel laureates, among them Peter Higgs and Sydney Brenner speaking about Frederick Sanger, have said that their work would not survive today's pressure to publish regularly.

The other side#

Not everything in science is a strong-link problem. Fraud, errors in data that others build on, and clinical results that guide treatment are weak-link problems: one bad result can do real harm. Dan Elton's defence of peer review, discussed in Understanding peer review, argues that expert screening also protects fields from spending years on false spectacular claims. The point is not to remove all quality control, but to know which kind of problem you face.

What it changes for your own work#

  • Aim for your best work, not for zero flaws. Your career will be remembered for its best results. Ten safe papers matter less than one important one.
  • Allow yourself risky projects. Keep a safe project that will surely produce a paper, and a risky one that might produce something important.
  • Spend little energy policing others. Pointing out errors that matter is part of science. Spending a career fighting mediocre work changes little. Doing great work changes more.
  • Judge a researcher by their best work. In hiring, refereeing and collaborating, ask what someone's strongest result is.
  • Know when you face a weak link. When others will rely on your data, your code or your proof, check it as if one error could ruin everything, because it can.

Checklist#

  • I can name the most important result I am working towards, not only the next paper.
  • I have at least one risky project alongside a safe one.
  • I check with extra care every result that others will build on.
  • When I evaluate others, I look first at their best work.

Summary#

  • Weak-link problems depend on the worst element, strong-link problems on the best.
  • Science is mostly a strong-link problem: its best ideas last and the rest is forgotten.
  • Screening for safety can also remove the most original work.
  • Aim high, take some risks, and check carefully what others will build on.
