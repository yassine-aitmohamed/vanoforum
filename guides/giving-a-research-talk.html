Giving a research talk

How to plan, build and deliver a research talk. Know the audience, follow the three-part structure, design clear slides or board work, rehearse, and handle questions.

A talk is not a paper read aloud. A good talk gives the audience one main idea, one main result, and the feeling that they understand why it matters. The details are in the paper.

Know your audience

Before writing anything, ask: who will be in the room?

  • Departmental colloquium: researchers from all areas. Most of the talk must be accessible.
  • Specialised seminar: people from your area. You can go further, but still start slowly.
  • Conference talk: often 20 to 30 minutes. One result, one idea of proof.
  • Job talk: a mix of general audience and experts. Show the big picture and your independence.

The three-part structure

A classic rule divides the talk into three parts:

  1. For everyone (about a third). The context, the question, why it matters, an example everyone can follow.
  2. For the field (about a third). The precise statement of the results, how they compare with earlier work.
  3. For the experts (the rest). An idea of the proof, the main new ingredient.

Every listener should leave with something. Most people will remember only the first part. Make it good.

Plan from the end

Write down the one sentence you want people to remember. Then build the talk backwards: what does the audience need to know to understand that sentence? Remove everything else.

Slides or board

  • Board talks force a slower pace, which helps the audience follow. They are common in mathematics.
  • Slides let you show pictures, diagrams and longer statements. Do not put too much on each slide.

For slides:

  • One idea per slide.
  • Large font. Few words. No paragraphs.
  • State theorems in a short, readable form. Hide technical conditions or move them to a remark.
  • Use pictures and diagrams.
  • Number slides so people can ask about a specific one.
  • Plan about one slide per one to two minutes.

Rehearse

  • Rehearse aloud, standing, with a timer, at least twice.
  • Give a practice talk to your group and ask for honest comments.
  • Prepare a version that can be shortened by five minutes if the session runs late. Know which slides you can skip.

Delivering the talk

  • Speak slowly and look at the audience.
  • Repeat the main question and the main result.
  • Give examples before general statements.
  • Finish on time. Running over is one of the few things audiences never forgive.
  • End with a summary slide that stays on screen during questions.

Handling questions

  • Repeat the question so everyone can hear it.
  • If you do not know the answer, say so and offer to discuss it afterwards.
  • If a question is long or technical, suggest talking after the session.
  • Thank people for their questions.

Checklist

  • I know the audience and adapted the level.
  • My talk has a part for everyone, a part for the field, a part for experts.
  • I can state the main message in one sentence.
  • I rehearsed aloud with a timer.
  • I know which slides to skip if time is short.
