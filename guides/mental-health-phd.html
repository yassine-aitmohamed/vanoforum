Mental health during the PhD

Depression and anxiety are common among doctoral students, and much of the risk comes from how the work is organised. Know the evidence, recognise the warning signs early, protect what helps, and ask for help before things become serious.

A doctorate combines long uncertainty, isolation, high standards, financial pressure and an unclear future. It is not surprising that many doctoral students struggle. It is important to know that this is common, that it is not a personal failure, and that help works best when it comes early. This guide is general information, not medical advice. If you are struggling, talk to a professional.

What the evidence says#

  • A study of 3,659 doctoral students in Flanders, Belgium, published in Research Policy in 2017, found that about one in two experienced psychological distress and about one in three was at risk of a common psychiatric disorder, especially depression. These rates were higher than in comparable highly educated groups outside academia.
  • A systematic review and meta-analysis published in Scientific Reports in 2021 pooled studies from several countries. It estimated that about 24% of doctoral students have clinically significant symptoms of depression and about 17% of anxiety, with wide variation between studies.

These numbers are estimates from surveys, and they vary by country, field and method. They show one thing clearly: if you are struggling, you are far from alone.

What increases the risk#

The Belgian study looked at which working conditions go with mental health problems. The strongest links were:

  • conflict between work and family or private life,
  • high demands combined with little control over one's own work,
  • the supervisor's leadership style,
  • the decision-making culture of the team,
  • a pessimistic view of careers outside academia.

Many of these are features of the environment, not of the person. Some can be changed: how meetings with the supervisor work, how much control you have over your schedule, whether you know that careers outside academia exist and are valued.

Warning signs#

Pay attention when these last for more than two weeks:

  • persistent low mood, emptiness or irritability,
  • loss of interest in research and in things you used to enjoy,
  • sleeping much more or much less than usual,
  • constant worry, tension or panic,
  • difficulty concentrating, even on simple tasks,
  • withdrawing from colleagues, friends and family,
  • feeling worthless or that things will not get better.

These signs are a reason to talk to a professional, not a judgment on your ability as a researcher.

What helps#

  • Talk early. To a friend, another doctoral student, a mentor, your supervisor if you trust them. Saying it aloud often changes how heavy it feels.
  • Use professional help. Most universities offer free and confidential counselling for students. A general practitioner or family doctor is another starting point. Effective treatments exist.
  • Protect sleep, movement and meals. They are the base everything else rests on.
  • Keep a life outside research. Friends, sport, family, a hobby. Your identity is more than your thesis.
  • Reduce isolation. Work some days in a shared space, join a reading group, go to seminars.
  • Clarify expectations. Many worries come from not knowing what is expected. The guide What a PhD expects from you lists the questions to ask your supervisor.
  • Know your options. A leave of absence, a change of supervisor or project, or stopping the PhD are legitimate choices. Doctoral schools have contacts for difficulties with supervision.

If you are in crisis#

If you have thoughts of harming yourself, or feel unable to keep yourself safe, contact your local emergency number or a crisis line now. Your university counselling service or doctor can also help you find immediate support. You do not have to wait until things get worse.

For supervisors and colleagues#

Ask how people are doing, and listen to the answer. Make it normal to talk about difficulties. Know where your university's support services are, so that you can point to them.

Checklist#

  • I know where my university's counselling service is and how to contact it.
  • I have at least one person I can talk to honestly about how I am doing.
  • I sleep, move and eat regularly most weeks.
  • I have a life outside research that I protect.
  • I know the warning signs, and I will ask for help if they last more than two weeks.

Summary#

  • Depression and anxiety are common among doctoral students.
  • Much of the risk comes from working conditions, not from personal weakness.
  • Learn the warning signs, and ask for help early.
  • Sleep, people and a life outside research are the base.

Further reading#

The figures in this guide come from two research articles:

  • Katia Levecque and coauthors, Work organization and mental health problems in PhD students, Research Policy 46 (2017).
  • Emily N. Satinsky and coauthors, Systematic review and meta-analysis of depression, anxiety, and suicidal ideation among Ph.D. students, Scientific Reports 11 (2021).
