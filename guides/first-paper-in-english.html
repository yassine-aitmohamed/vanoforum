Writing your first paper in English

The anatomy of a research paper, the order in which to write it, and the sentence-level habits that make English academic prose clear, even when English is not your first language.

Many researchers write their first paper in a language that is not their own. This is not a weakness. Clear academic English is a small, learnable language: short sentences, precise words, and a fixed set of conventions. Elegance comes later. Clarity comes first.

The anatomy of a paper#

Every section answers one question for the reader.

Part The question it answers
Title What is this paper about?
Abstract What does it prove or find, in a few sentences?
Introduction Why does it matter, what is new, and what are the main results?
Preliminaries What does the reader need to know, and with which notation?
Main sections How are the results proved or obtained?
Examples or applications What do the results give in concrete cases?
References What does the paper build on?

A reader decides from the title whether to read the abstract, from the abstract whether to read the introduction, and from the introduction whether to read the rest. Most of your readers will stop somewhere along the way. Write each part so that it is useful even if the reader stops there.

The order in which to write#

Do not write the paper in reading order.

  1. The results. Write the precise statements of the main results first, with their hypotheses. Everything else is organized around them.
  2. The proofs and the body. Write the technical sections. Notation will change while you work. That is normal.
  3. The preliminaries. Now you know exactly which definitions and known results the body uses. Include those, and only those.
  4. The introduction. Now you know what the paper really does.
  5. The abstract and the title. Last, when the content is fixed.

Before you write: discuss the strategy#

Before writing a single formal proof, write half a page that explains the strategy: what the key idea is, which known results it relies on, and where the difficulty lies. Discuss it with your supervisor or a co-author. A wrong strategy found at this stage costs an afternoon. Found after twenty pages of LaTeX, it costs weeks.

Sentence-level habits#

Short and direct sentences. One idea per sentence. If a sentence has three clauses, try splitting it. Semicolons are rarely needed in mathematical prose. A period is almost always better.

Subject, verb, object. "We prove that the map is injective" is better than "It is the injectivity of the map that is proved here."

Use "we". In mathematics and many sciences, "we" is standard and natural: "we prove", "we show", "we construct". Refer to your own earlier papers in the same way ("in [3], we showed"), not as "the author".

Strong, precise verbs. Prefer prove, construct, classify, characterize, determine, establish, extend to vague verbs like study, consider, deal with, look at.

Define before you use. Every object and every symbol must be defined before its first use, and defined only once. If a definition appears in the introduction, do not repeat it in Section 2. Refer back to it.

One notation, kept fixed. Choose your notation once and keep it in the whole paper. Use the standard notation of your field when it exists. Do not rename objects without a reason.

Cite precisely. Write "by [12, Theorem 4.3]", not "by a result in [12]". The reader should be able to find the exact statement in seconds.

Do not reprove what is known. If a result is in the literature, cite it with its exact number and use it. Do not restate it as a new Proposition and do not reprove it.

Avoid empty phrases. Cut "It is worth noting that", "It is well known that" (when it is not), "In this regard", "Moreover, it should be emphasized that". These add length and remove clarity.

Small grammar points that matter#

  • Articles. "A" introduces a new object, "the" refers to one already known. "Let $G$ be a group. The group $G$ acts on…".
  • Tense. Use the present for mathematics and for what the paper does ("We prove", "Theorem A shows"). Use the past for history ("Weinstein proved in 1983").
  • "Which" and "that". "The map that we construct" (restrictive, no comma). "The map, which is smooth, …" (additional information, with commas).
  • Do not start a sentence with a symbol. Write "The group $G$ is abelian", not "$G$ is abelian" at the start of a sentence.
  • Hyphens. "A well-known result" before a noun, but "the result is well known" after the verb.

Learning from good writing#

Choose three or four papers in your field that you find very clear. Read them again, this time for the language. Copy into a notebook the phrases you like: how they announce a result, how they introduce a definition, how they begin a proof. This personal phrasebook will serve you for years.

A checklist before sending the draft to anyone#

  • Each main result is stated precisely, with all its hypotheses.
  • Every symbol is defined before its first use, and only once.
  • Every cited result has an exact reference number.
  • No known result is reproved.
  • Each section begins with a sentence that states its goal.
  • The notation is the same in every section.
  • The abstract and the introduction match what the paper actually proves.
