The introduction is where the paper is accepted or rejected. A referee forms an opinion in the first two pages. A reader decides whether to continue. It must position the problem, motivate it, and make the contribution impossible to miss.

## The arc of a good introduction

1. **Context and history.** Where does the problem come from? Who introduced the objects, and what was known? A few sentences of history give the reader a frame. Do not cut them to save space.
2. **Motivation.** Why does the question matter? What does it unlock, what does it explain, which open problem does it touch? This is the *why* of the paper, and it must be explicit.
3. **The gap.** What was missing before this paper? Be fair to earlier work. The gap is what nobody had done, not what others did badly.
4. **The main contribution.** State it early, ideally on the first or second page. If the paper has one main theorem, it should appear before the reader has to turn too many pages.
5. **The other results.** Describe the contribution of each part of the paper in general terms. Not every result needs to be stated in full in the introduction.
6. **Organization of the paper.** A short paragraph at the end, one sentence per section.
7. **Acknowledgments.** Thank the people and institutions who helped.

## Stating the main results

- **Label the main results** Theorem A, Theorem B, Theorem C in the introduction, so they are easy to cite and easy to find. Keep the numbered Theorem, Proposition and Lemma environments for the body.
- **Define everything first.** Every object and every symbol in Theorem A must be defined before Theorem A. Introduce the objects in the prose that leads to the theorem.
- **Never mention a theorem before stating it.** Do not write "Theorem B below shows…" in the first paragraph. State Theorem B, then discuss it.
- **Keep statements minimal.** The statement should contain the hypotheses and the conclusion, nothing else. Interpretations, special cases and remarks go in the prose right after the statement.
- **Keep the subtleties for later.** Sign conventions, technical normalizations and degenerate cases belong to the definitions in the body. The introduction states the clean version.

## What to leave out

- **Details of the proofs.** One or two sentences on the main idea of a proof are welcome when the idea is itself new. A step-by-step account of the proof is not. It belongs to the body.
- **Long lists of internal references.** "By Lemma 3.4, Proposition 4.2 and Corollary 5.7" makes the introduction unreadable. The reader has not seen these yet.
- **Every reference you know.** Cite what is essential to position the problem and what the paper actually uses. An introduction is not a survey.
- **Thematic subsections.** A research introduction usually reads best as continuous prose, with only the final "Organization" and "Acknowledgments" as separate headings.
- **The same first sentence as in your last three papers.** If every introduction you write opens with "X, introduced by Y in 19…, have been widely studied", change it. Open with the question, a striking example, or the reason the problem matters now.

## The motivation paragraph

A motivation paragraph answers *why should anyone care?* Three good answers:

- **It solves or advances a known problem**: an open question, a conjecture, a gap explicitly mentioned in the literature. Cite where it was asked.
- **It connects two areas**: the result lets methods from one field act on another.
- **It explains a phenomenon**: it shows why a family of known examples behaves the way it does.

A bad answer is "this has not been studied before". Many things have not been studied, for good reasons.

## Selling the paper, honestly

The introduction must sell the paper, but with mathematical honesty. Say clearly what is new. If your theorem extends a known result, explain what the extension adds: more generality, a new method, a sharp bound, a counterexample showing the hypotheses are needed. Do not make your results look like routine special cases, and do not claim more than you prove.

## Checklist

- [ ] The first page says what the problem is and why it matters.
- [ ] The history is short but present.
- [ ] The main result appears early.
- [ ] Every object in a main theorem is defined before it.
- [ ] No theorem is mentioned before it is stated.
- [ ] No proof is explained in detail.
- [ ] Only essential references are cited.
- [ ] The last paragraphs are "Organization of the paper" and "Acknowledgments".
