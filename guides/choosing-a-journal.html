Choosing a journal

Scope first, then level, then the editor. What Q1 and Q2 really mean, how to read an editorial board, how to recognize a predatory journal, and how to build a realistic list of targets.

Choosing a journal is a strategic decision. A good choice gives a fair review by people who understand the work and a publication that the right readers will see. A bad choice costs months: a desk rejection, a referee outside the field, or a paper lost in a journal nobody reads.

Step 1: scope#

The first question is not "how good is the journal?" but "does this journal publish papers like mine?"

  • Read the aims and scope on the journal's page.
  • Look at the last two years of issues. Are there papers on your topic, with your methods?
  • Look at your own bibliography. Where were the papers you cite most published? Those journals already have editors and referees for your area.

A paper outside a journal's scope is often rejected by the editor without review, however good it is.

Step 2: level#

Be honest about the significance of your result. Ask a senior colleague or your supervisor. A useful way to think:

  • Top general journals publish results that matter to a wide part of the discipline.
  • Strong specialized journals publish important results within one area.
  • Good solid journals publish correct, useful, new results with a narrower audience.

Aim one notch higher than your first instinct, not three. A realistic ladder of three journals, decided before the first submission, saves time after a rejection.

What Q1, Q2, Q3 and Q4 mean#

Quartiles rank journals within a subject category by a citation indicator.

  • Q1 means the journal is in the top 25% of its category, Q2 in the next 25%, and so on.
  • The two common sources are SCImago Journal Rank (SJR, free, at scimagojr.com, based on Scopus data) and Clarivate's Journal Citation Reports (JCR, by subscription, based on the Journal Impact Factor).
  • The same journal can be Q1 in one category and Q2 in another, and can have different quartiles in SJR and JCR. Always say which ranking you use.
  • Quartiles change every year.

Use quartiles carefully. They measure citations, and citation habits vary enormously between fields. In mathematics, papers are cited slowly and over decades, so citation metrics are weak signals. Mathematicians also look at MathSciNet's Mathematical Citation Quotient (MCQ) and above all at the reputation of the journal among specialists. Your institution or funding agency may require Q1 or Q2 publications. Know the rule, but choose the journal for its readers, not only for its quartile.

Step 3: the editor#

The editor-in-chief usually does not handle your paper personally. A member of the editorial board does: the handling editor, sometimes called associate editor. This person chooses the referees and makes the recommendation.

  • Read the editorial board. Is there at least one editor whose own research is close to your paper? If nobody on the board works in your area, the journal is probably not the right one.
  • Some journals ask you to choose or suggest an editor. Choose the board member whose recent papers are closest to yours, not the most famous one.
  • Suggested referees. Many journals let you suggest referees. Suggest experts without conflicts of interest: no co-authors, no colleagues from your institution, no close collaborators.

Step 4: practical criteria#

  • Review time. Some journals publish median times from submission to decision. In mathematics, a full review often takes several months and can take more than a year.
  • Open access and fees. Diamond open access journals charge neither readers nor authors. Gold open access journals charge an article processing charge (APC). Subscription journals are free for authors and usually allow you to keep the preprint on arXiv. Check the journal's copyright and preprint policy.
  • Indexing. Check that the journal is indexed where your field looks: Web of Science, Scopus, zbMATH Open and MathSciNet for mathematics, PubMed for life sciences.
  • Length and format limits. Some journals have page limits or require a specific template.

Recognizing a predatory journal#

Predatory journals charge fees and publish almost anything, with little or no real review. They damage a CV. Warning signs:

  • An unsolicited email inviting you to submit, often flattering, often outside your field.
  • A promise of very fast acceptance, "peer review in 7 days".
  • A broad, vague scope ("International Journal of Advanced Science and Research").
  • Fees announced only after acceptance, or hard to find.
  • An editorial board that is hard to verify, or names used without consent.
  • Invented metrics ("Global Impact Factor").

Use the checklist at thinkchecksubmit.org. For open access journals, check that the journal is listed in the Directory of Open Access Journals (doaj.org). Ask your supervisor or librarian when in doubt.

Rules you must follow#

  • One journal at a time. Submitting the same paper to two journals simultaneously is not allowed.
  • Posting on arXiv before or during submission is accepted by most journals in mathematics, physics and computer science. Check the policy of your target journal.
  • After a rejection, read the reports, improve the paper, and submit to the next journal on your list. Do not send the same version again.

Checklist#

  • The journal publishes papers like mine, in the last two years.
  • The level matches the significance of the result.
  • At least one editor works in my area.
  • The journal is indexed and is not predatory.
  • I know its fees, preprint policy and typical review time.
  • I have a list of three journals, in order.
