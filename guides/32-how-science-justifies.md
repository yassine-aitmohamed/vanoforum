Ask a scientist how science works and you will often hear Karl Popper: a theory can never be proved, but a single observation can refute it, so science advances by trying to falsify theories. The picture is attractive and partly right. It is also, as philosophers of science have argued for decades, incomplete in ways that matter for anyone who does research. This guide summarises the main ideas and what they change in practice.

## The logic of testing

A scientific prediction has the form: if the theory $T$ is true, we will observe $O$. Two inferences look natural.

- **We observe $O$, so $T$ is true.** This is affirming the consequent, a logical fallacy. Yet almost all experimental science reasons like this: the prediction came true, so the theory gains credit.
- **We do not observe $O$, so $T$ is false.** This is *modus tollens*, a valid deduction, the same rule as a proof by contradiction.

Popper built his philosophy on the second inference only. Since no number of confirmed predictions can prove a theory, he concluded that science should not try to confirm theories at all, only to refute them. Theories that survive severe tests are kept, but in his view they never become more likely to be true.

## Why falsification is not enough

- **Theories are tested with assumptions.** A theory alone predicts nothing observable. Newton's laws predict planetary orbits only with assumptions about the masses and forces involved. When a prediction fails, logic tells you that something in the whole package is wrong, not which part. This is the Duhem–Quine thesis. Scientists therefore often keep the theory and revise an assumption, sometimes rightly: the planet Neptune was found that way.
- **Many theories are probabilistic.** Quantum mechanics predicts probabilities. No single observation can deduce that a probability is false. Yet quantum mechanics is a paradigm of science.
- **Evidence comes in degrees.** The crater at Chicxulub does not logically prove that an asteroid killed the dinosaurs, but it makes the hypothesis much more likely. Most scientific evidence works like this.

The philosopher Michael Huemer argues that the right framework is probabilistic. Evidence $E$ supports a hypothesis $H$ when $H$ is more probable given $E$ than without it. A short computation shows that $E$ supports $H$ exactly when the absence of $E$ would count against $H$. This explains why testability matters: a theory that no observation could count against can receive no support from any observation either.

## Theories live in programmes

Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) described how science actually proceeds. Most of the time, researchers work inside a shared framework, a paradigm, and treat anomalies as puzzles to solve, not as refutations. Only when anomalies accumulate does the framework change. Imre Lakatos described the same history as competing research programmes: a programme stays alive while it keeps predicting new facts, and degenerates when it only patches its failures.

For a researcher, the lesson is practical. A single counterexample to your approach does not mean you must abandon it. A long series of patches with no new results is a sign that you should.

## Where mathematics stands

Mathematics is the one field where theorems are proved by deduction. But mathematics is not discovered by deduction. Lakatos's *Proofs and Refutations* (1976) follows the history of Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ for polyhedra. A conjecture is proposed, counterexamples appear, the definitions are adjusted, a proof is attempted, the proof reveals hidden hypotheses, and the statement is refined again.

This is the daily practice of research mathematics:

1. **Examples** suggest a pattern.
2. **A conjecture** states it precisely.
3. **Counterexamples** test it. Each one is a falsification in Popper's sense.
4. **A proof attempt** shows which hypotheses are really used.
5. **The statement is refined** until the proof goes through.

The final paper shows only steps 2 and 5. The research happens in between.

## What this changes for you

- **Say what would change your mind.** For every claim, know which observation, example or counterexample would count against it.
- **Separate the theory from its assumptions.** When something fails, list every assumption in the chain before deciding what to drop.
- **Report evidence in degrees.** Distinguish what is proved, what is strongly supported, and what is a guess.
- **Judge programmes, not single results.** Ask whether your approach still produces new results, or only explains away its failures.

## Checklist

- [ ] For my main conjecture, I know what kind of example would refute it.
- [ ] When an approach failed, I listed every assumption it used.
- [ ] My paper separates proved results, supported claims and conjectures.
- [ ] I have tested my conjecture on the smallest and most degenerate cases.

## Summary

- A failed prediction refutes a package of theory and assumptions, not a theory alone.
- Evidence supports theories in degrees. A theory that nothing could count against cannot be supported either.
- Science advances in programmes, judged by whether they keep producing new results.
- Mathematics proves by deduction but discovers by examples, conjectures and counterexamples.
